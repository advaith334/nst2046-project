# Strengthening the evidence base (report draft, sections 6 and 7)

Prepared 5 Nov 2026 against `report/draft/report.md` as saved at 15:27. Line numbers (L161 etc.) refer to that file. Nothing in the draft was changed. All sources are paraphrased; page references let the team pull exact wording.

## Bottom line

- **Justice premise: holds in a narrower form.** There is a real gradient in *what people know* on settled facts. Formal education predicts very little about *misinformation discernment* or *contested, identity-laden beliefs*. Literacy, numeracy, subject knowledge, trust and class do the work.
- **Our item type sits at the strong end** (settled facts). But three links stay unproven: ignorance is not false belief, less-educated people use chatbots less overall, and nobody has measured whose false beliefs get stored.
- **All five in-text [CHECK SOURCE] markers and their five reference flags are resolved:** FRE 803(3) confirmed and tightened, Singapore confirmed and made precise, Elish confirmed with a limit, Microsoft confirmed with a new fact (Copilot Health is currently offline), and Pugh **corrected** (it does not say patients are often right).
- **Fricker:** the 2017 chapter is now read whole. The 2007 book is still unread, but its key passages are now seen quoted with page numbers in Lackey (2018). "Credibility economy" is a chapter title, and no text we read defines it.
- **Best new philosophical point:** a belief can be some evidence for a model, but it is never new evidence for the person who already holds it (Kelly; Nguyen). This repairs the weakest step in "category error" and changes how the true-memory gain should be read.

## 1. The justice premise

**Draft premise (L177):** false beliefs on checkable facts are not randomly spread but follow unequal access to education and expert advice.

### What the premise can and cannot carry

| Link | Status |
|---|---|
| 1. False beliefs on settled facts follow social position | **Supported**, through skills and class more than years of schooling |
| 2. Those beliefs get *stated and stored* | **Unmeasured.** Pew and Pan score "not sure" as wrong, so they measure ignorance as well as false belief. KFF's 2023 pilot (SNIP) found most adults unsure rather than convinced. Memory needs a stated belief |
| 3. Those people use memory-enabled assistants | **Mixed.** Lower overall use; higher among low health literacy users in one Australian sample |
| 4. Memory widens answers by belief | **Ours**, one model, general facts |
| 5. Unequal answers become unequal outcomes | **Unmeasured** |

**A route that avoids link 1.** Poole-Dayan et al. (repo notes, not re-read) report worse answers for users with lower English proficiency or less education. Goddard et al. report less experienced users more affected by automation bias. Neither needs the premise.

**Best one-sentence wording:**

> On settled factual topics false beliefs are not randomly spread: they follow literacy, numeracy, subject knowledge and trust, which are themselves unequally distributed by education and class, though years of schooling alone predicts little on misinformation or contested claims.

### Justice paragraph (L175)

> **Justice.** Fricker's norm is that a hearer should match credibility to the evidence that a speaker is offering the truth (2007, p. 19, as quoted in Lackey, 2018). Nemo departs from that norm in both directions: it gives a false belief more weight than the evidence warrants, and the label gives a true one less. We do not claim testimonial injustice. Her central case is a credibility deficit caused by identity prejudice (2007, p. 28, as quoted in Lackey, 2018), and we varied no identity. Excess would not qualify in any case, since she holds that excess does a speaker no epistemic injustice in itself and can harm only by accumulating into arrogance (2007, pp. 20–21, as quoted in Lackey, 2018). Medina (2011, as quoted in Lackey, 2018) replies that this looks only at single exchanges, and that credibility is comparative. Her distributive sense fits better: someone receives "less than their fair share of an epistemic good", such as information (Fricker, 2017). The good at issue here is a correct answer, not credibility, which she denies is a distributive good.

### Premise paragraph (L177, from "The argument then needs")

> The argument then needs one stated premise, and the evidence supports a narrower version than we first assumed. On settled factual topics false beliefs are not randomly spread: they follow literacy, numeracy, subject knowledge and trust, which are themselves unequally distributed by education and class (Hwang & Jeong, 2009; Nan et al., 2022). In Singapore the literacy gap by education is wider than the OECD average (OECD, 2024), and in one sample of GP patients 96% with primary education or less failed to reject the claim that antibiotics work on viruses, against 75% with post-secondary education (Pan et al., 2016). Years of schooling alone predicts little on misinformation (Sultan et al., 2024; Soon & Goh, 2021), and on contested topics the educated can be more polarised (Drummond & Fischhoff, 2017). So memory may convert unequal prior knowledge into unequal answers on settled facts, which is where our items sit. Three links remain unmeasured: surveys often score "not sure" as wrong, while a memory needs a stated belief; less-educated people use chatbots less overall (Pew, 2025), although limited health literacy was commoner among Australian health users (Ayre et al., 2025); and nobody has measured whose false beliefs get stored.

### What the 2017 chapter says (first-hand)

- Epistemic injustice in general: being downgraded or disadvantaged in one's status as an epistemic subject.
- Testimonial injustice is caused by prejudice, and is an ingenuous misjudgement, never deliberate. She asks for strictness so the category does not expand to cover all unfair manipulation or systemic rigging of the epistemic economy.
- The distributive sense is credited to Coady. A footnote says the discriminatory/distributive line is not deep: each can cause the other.
- Both kinds wrong someone in their capacity as an epistemic subject. That line supports the draft's use.
- A functional practice contains counter-pressures against anti-veridical forces. This is a useful frame for harness fixes.
- Healthcare is named as a live application (Carel & Kidd; delusion assessment). This supports the patient case.
- Structural testimonial injustice can exist without anyone at fault, as with Anderson's unrefreshed list of expert witnesses. A possible lead for stale records, but it needs prejudice in the structure, so use with care.

**The chapter never uses "credibility excess", "credibility deficit" or "credibility economy".** It uses "epistemic economy" once and speaks of credibility relations.

### Does excess count as testimonial injustice for Fricker?

**Not in itself.** She allows it may be disadvantageous but says it does not withhold proper respect for the speaker as a knower, so it is no testimonial injustice (p. 20). No single moment of excess wrongs the person enough (p. 21). The only route is cumulative: repeated excess may produce arrogance and a closed mind (pp. 20–21). The primary characterisation stays deficit, not excess (p. 21, VIA Whittaker). All VIA.

A search result claimed she later accepted prejudicial excess. The source (a Bristol reading list) returned 404, and the 2017 chapter does not address it. **Unverified.**

### Medina's reply (VIA Lackey and Whittaker)

- Fricker's "in itself" test is short-sighted because it looks only at single exchanges (2011, p. 16).
- He agrees credibility is not finite like food or water (p. 19).
- But credibility is comparative and contrastive: excess for some is tied to deficit for others, because each is judged against a normal subject (p. 20). His view is proportional.
- Excess can spoil the recipient's epistemic character (2013, p. 88, VIA Whittaker).

### Further reply: Lackey (2018), first-hand

- Excess can wrong immediately, when a stereotype casts someone as expert in a stigmatised domain.
- **Hearer excess:** a hearer who gives himself unwarranted credibility wrongs a speaker even while rating her correctly.
- Credibility is relational, and sometimes finite. But she says plain factual disagreement explained by innocent error does *not* make it finite. So her finitude point does **not** straightforwardly cover a stale record against a true patient.
- **Wide Norm:** match assessments of all relevant parties, including oneself, to the evidence one has and should have.

**Use for us.** The label plus a wrong prior resembles hearer excess: the system backs its own prior over a true report. There is no prejudice, so it is no injustice in her sense. This is exactly what the proposed matched-guise test would probe. "Evidence one should have" also fits deployers who never test.

### How the fit changes the argument

- Fricker's cumulative excess is the nearest match to a memory that keeps confirming someone, and the harm falls on the **over-credited user**. That reverses the worry that being over-believed cannot harm you. We tested single turns, so this is a hypothesis only.
- Fricker calls excess unusual as a harm. Do not overstate it.
- The draft's distributive move works only if the good is **correct answers**. Saying credibility itself is unfairly distributed contradicts her.

## 4. Philosophical strengthening

### Kelly (2005), "The epistemic significance of disagreement"

**Access:** FT-part, pp. 26–30 of a 36-page draft on a course site. Published page numbers not seen.

Kelly points out that someone listing their reasons for H lists the first-order evidence, not the fact that they believe H. So if I have your evidence and also count your belief, I am double-counting, much as an insurer who knows a driver's actual record should stop weighing age: the evidence screens off the belief. But if I lack your evidence, your belief can stand in as a proxy for it. He says himself that this line of thought is not decisive.

**Use.** This gives the "category error" its proper form and its proper limit. Where a model has no evidence of its own, a user's belief is a proxy and is reliable only as far as the user is. That explains why true memories help Nemo, and why the claim applies where knowledge is thin. The error is not using the belief at all, but letting it settle the question with no estimate of the user's reliability.

### Nguyen (2020), "Echo chambers and epistemic bubbles"

**Access:** FT-part, pp. 1–5 of the 21-page online version (Northeastern-hosted copy), extending the earlier agent's reading.

An epistemic bubble omits relevant voices, often without intent. Omission also threatens bootstrapped corroboration: agreement raises confidence, but corroboration should count only if it adds something and is not a mere copy, as with a stack of identical newspapers. He adds that the hidden circularity is easily forgotten because time and interface obscure it. Goldberg's coverage reliability (VIA Nguyen) names the related failure: relying on a system to surface what you need to know.

**Use.** A memory is a one-person version of this: the user's belief returns through a system, and the time gap and interface hide the source. This supports L173 and the finding that sources disappear from residual errors. Coverage gives a name for "the correction that never came": a user may read silence as a clean bill of health. It is a bubble, not an echo chamber, because nothing is discredited. The label does discount the user's voice, so test that point before using "echo chamber".

### Lackey (2018), "Credibility and the distribution of epistemic goods"

**Access:** FT-part (see section 3).

Summarised in section 3. It is the best single source for the report: it reproduces the key Fricker passages with page numbers, challenges the evidentialist norm directly, and treats credibility as relational across all parties, the hearer included.

**Use.** It supports the claim that a single dial on user credibility cannot work, since assessment must cover user, record, passage and the model's own prior together. It also gives the wording for the responsibility section: deployers are answerable for evidence they *should* have.

### Barnett (penultimate draft), "Belief dependence: how do the numbers count?"

**Access:** FT-part, pp. 1–5 of a 24-page penultimate draft, PhilArchive. Lackey's own chapter on this (2013) is not open.

The received view holds that a totally dependent opinion adds no support; Elga and Kelly both accept it (VIA Barnett). Lackey (2013) rejects it. Barnett defends it with a test: one opinion depends on another when knowing the second lets you predict the first. He also notes that outside opinion can be evidence when it is all you have.

**Use.** It gives a clean test: if an answer can be predicted from the record, the answer adds no support for the user. That could double as a pass mark for a memory system. Note that the principle is contested.
